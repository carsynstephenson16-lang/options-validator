## Topic A — moving averages / trend / momentum

### 1. Brock, Lakonishok & LeBaron (1992)
**Title:** "Simple Technical Trading Rules and the Stochastic Properties of Stock Returns"
**Authors:** William Brock, Josef Lakonishok, Blake LeBaron
**Venue:** The Journal of Finance, Vol. 47, No. 5 (Dec 1992), pp. 1731–1764. DOI: 10.1111/j.1540-6261.1992.tb04681.x
**URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1992.tb04681.x (also indexed at https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=125605)
**Finding:** Tested 26 variants of two simple technical rules — moving-average crossover and trading-range breakout (support/resistance) — on the Dow Jones Industrial Average, 1897–1986 (90 years), using bootstrap inference against four null models (random walk, AR(1), GARCH-M, EGARCH). Buy signals produced consistently higher, less volatile returns than sell signals, and the results were not consistent with (i.e., rejected) all four null models — an unusually strong result for such a long sample.
**Practical implication:** This is the foundational positive result for MA-based trend rules on a single, very long index series; it is 90+ years old, pre-transaction-cost, and on one index — it establishes that MA rules *can* carry real signal in raw returns, not that they survive costs or generalize to single-name equities/options, which is exactly what Sullivan/Timmermann/White (#2) interrogates next.

### 2. Sullivan, Timmermann & White (1999)
**Title:** "Data-Snooping, Technical Trading Rule Performance, and the Bootstrap"
**Authors:** Ryan Sullivan, Allan Timmermann, Halbert L. White Jr.
**Venue:** The Journal of Finance, Vol. 54, No. 5 (Oct 1999), pp. 1647–1691. DOI: 10.1111/0022-1082.00163
**URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/0022-1082.00163 (SSRN: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=65140; free LSE working-paper PDF: https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/119144/1/dp303.pdf)
**Finding:** Applied White's "Reality Check" bootstrap to the full universe of ~7,846 trading rules (expanding Brock/Lakonishok/LeBaron's 26-rule universe) on 100 years of DJIA data, explicitly quantifying and correcting for data-snooping bias across the whole search space. Over the original BLL sample period, the best rule still showed superior performance even after the full data-snooping correction — but the same rule's performance did not hold up out-of-sample in the post-1986 period they added, consistent with the effect decaying/being arbitraged away over time.
**Practical implication:** This is the direct rebuttal-with-nuance to item #1: technical rules "survive this test" in-sample even under a rigorous multiple-testing correction, but the out-of-sample decay is the real headline — a strong warning that any single MA rule discovered by scanning many variants needs an honest snooping-adjusted test (this repo's DSR/PBO tooling, #10 below, is the modern version of exactly this check) and that even a rule that clears it can still fail going forward.

### 3. Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000)
**Title:** "Foundations of Technical Analysis: Computational Algorithms, Statistical Inference, and Empirical Implementation"
**Authors:** Andrew W. Lo, Harry Mamaysky, Jiang Wang
**Venue:** The Journal of Finance, Vol. 55, No. 4 (Aug 2000), pp. 1705–1765. DOI: 10.1111/0022-1082.00265. Originally NBER Working Paper 7613.
**URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/0022-1082.00265 ; https://www.nber.org/papers/w7613
**Finding:** Proposed a systematic, automated version of chart-pattern recognition (head-and-shoulders, double tops/bottoms, etc.) using nonparametric kernel regression to smooth price series and detect pattern geometry objectively, rather than relying on a human's subjective eyeballing. Applied to a large panel of U.S. stocks (1962–1996), they found that several of these algorithmically detected patterns carried incremental predictive information for returns, with the effect notably stronger for Nasdaq stocks than NYSE/AMEX stocks.
**Practical implication:** Different from MA rules — this is about chart-pattern detection, not moving averages — and its relevance to a 4-name mega-cap/AI-infra options book is limited (weakest effects were in the most liquid, most efficiently priced names, which is exactly this program's universe); it is useful mainly as evidence that *any* technical-analysis claim needs an objective, algorithmic definition and out-of-sample test before being trusted, which is already this repo's standing discipline.

### 4. Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012)
**Title:** "Time Series Momentum"
**Authors:** Tobias J. Moskowitz, Yao Hua Ooi, Lasse Heje Pedersen
**Venue:** Journal of Financial Economics, Vol. 104, No. 2 (2012), pp. 228–250. 
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X11002613 ; AQR mirror: https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Time-Series-Momentum ; free PDF: https://w4.stern.nyu.edu/facdir/lpederse/papers/TimeSeriesMomentum.pdf
**Finding:** Across 58 liquid futures/forwards (equity indices, currencies, commodities, sovereign bonds) over 25+ years, an asset's own past 12-month excess return positively predicts its next 1–12 month return, then that continuation partially reverses over longer (12+ month) horizons — a pattern the authors read as consistent with initial under-reaction followed by delayed over-reaction. The 12-month time-series momentum effect was positive not just on average across assets but individually for every one of the 58 instruments tested, and a diversified portfolio across all of them delivered large abnormal returns with low loadings on standard risk factors, performing best in extreme/crisis markets. Explicitly distinct from Jegadeesh-Titman cross-sectional momentum (which ranks assets against each other) — TSMOM compares each asset only to its own past.
**Practical implication:** This is the primary academic anchor for "12 months" as a lookback with real, broad, long-sample support — but it is a futures/cross-asset result, not single-name equity options; the diversification-across-58-instruments is doing real work in the Sharpe ratio, which a 4-name book cannot replicate, so the lookback-length finding transfers more credibly than the risk-adjusted-return magnitude does.

### 5. Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen
**Title:** "A Century of Evidence on Trend-Following Investing"
**Authors:** Brian Hurst, Yao Hua Ooi, Lasse Heje Pedersen
**Venue:** The Journal of Portfolio Management, Vol. 44, No. 1 (2017), pp. 15–29 (updates a 2012 AQR white paper).
**URL:** https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing ; SSRN: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2993026
**Finding:** Extends TSMOM testing back to 1880 across 67 markets (29 commodities, 11 equity indices, 15 bond markets, 12 currency pairs) through 2016, volatility-targeted to 10% annualized. Every decade since 1880 shows positive average trend-following returns with low correlation to stocks/bonds; the strategy performed well in 8 of the 10 largest 60/40-portfolio drawdown ("crisis") periods over the century, and was robust across recessions/booms, war/peacetime, and high/low interest-rate and inflation regimes, net of estimated implementation costs.
**Practical implication:** Reinforces #4 with much longer history and explicit crisis-alpha framing, but it is an AQR-authored, multi-century, multi-asset, volatility-targeted managed-futures result — the "trend-following works over 140 years" claim is about a diversified macro/futures overlay, and should not be read as direct evidence that a single-name options MA/momentum overlay on 4 mega-cap names will behave the same way (no crisis-alpha diversification benefit with n=4 correlated AI-infra names).

### 6. Levine & Pedersen (2016) — MOST IMPORTANT ITEM
**Title:** "Which Trend Is Your Friend?"
**Authors:** Ari Levine, Lasse Heje Pedersen
**Venue:** Financial Analysts Journal, Vol. 72, No. 3 (2016), pp. 51–66. DOI: 10.2469/faj.v72.n3.3
**URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.2469/faj.v72.n3.3 ; AQR: https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Which-Trend-Is-Your-Friend ; SSRN (abstract, full PDF gated): https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2603731 ; CFA Institute: https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2016/which-trend-is-your-friend ; open-access publisher PDF (CBS repository, binary/non-text-extractable via this session's tools): https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/60084063/lasse_heje_pedersen_et_al_which_trend_is_your_friend_publishersversion.pdf
**VERIFICATION NOTE:** I was not able to get full-text extraction — the SSRN and CFA Institute HTML pages both returned HTTP 403 to WebFetch, and the open-access CBS PDF fetched but returned as unparseable binary through this session's WebFetch tool (font/stream-encoded, not extractable as text). Everything below is therefore built from search-engine result summaries (multiple independent secondary sources — AQR's own summary, a CBS abstract page, and a third-party blog "Learning From AQR's Which Trend is Your Friend") rather than a direct read of the paper's body text or Table 1's actual numbers. This should be labeled **Inference**, not Official-source, until someone reads the PDF directly (e.g., via a PDF-text tool rather than WebFetch).
**Finding (as characterized across sources):** The paper's central claim is that two of the most common trend-following heuristics — time-series momentum (TSMOM, computed from past *returns* over a lookback window) and moving-average crossover (MACROSS, computed from past *price levels*, e.g., fast MA vs. slow MA) — are mathematically equivalent in their most general parametrized forms, and that this same general framework also nests the Hodrick-Prescott filter, the Kalman filter, and "all other linear filters" as special cases. They introduce "trend signature plots" to visualize how much weight each method places on price/return data at each lag/horizon, which lets you directly compare, e.g., a 10/50-day EMA crossover against an N-month TSMOM signal on equal footing. Their empirical work reuses the same 58-instrument Moskowitz-Ooi-Pedersen universe. Multiple secondary summaries describe a "Table 1" showing broadly similar Sharpe ratios for TSMOM and MACROSS across a range of speed parameters, and one summary states explicitly that "performance is relatively stable across a broad range of speed/lookback selections rather than being highly sensitive to a single 'optimal' parameter choice" — but I could not independently confirm the exact numbers behind that characterization from primary text, so treat the *qualitative* equivalence result (Official-source-adjacent, corroborated by 4+ independent summaries) as much more solid than the specific *quantitative* "flat across speeds" claim (Inference, single-thread corroboration).
**What the paper is reported to say about choosing EMA speed, specifically:** the practical conclusion multiple secondary sources converge on is that because MACROSS and TSMOM (and HP/Kalman filters) are shown to be near-equivalent and performance across their sensible parameterizations is similar, *which exact filter/speed you pick matters less than getting implementation right* — cost management, position sizing/vol targeting, diversification across signals, and risk management are where the argument says the real edge is won or lost, not fine-tuning the crossover speed to a precise optimum.
**Practical implication for this program:** if this characterization holds up under a direct primary-source read (recommended before leaning on it for a frozen parameter choice), it argues against grid-searching EMA fast/slow pairs to chase a backtest optimum on 4 names — that is exactly the overfitting risk this repo's DSR/PBO and pre-registration discipline exist to catch — and argues for picking one or two economically standard EMA pairs (e.g., something in the 10–50-day and 50–200-day families used across the cited literature) and freezing them, spending optimization effort on costs/fills/position-sizing instead.

### 7. EMA-pair (fast/slow) selection robustness — additional evidence
**VERIFICATION STATUS: partially verified, no single clean academic citation found beyond item #6.** What I found:
- The Levine & Pedersen (2016) Table-1-type comparison above (Inference-level, see #6) is the closest thing to a direct academic robustness result — "flat-ish Sharpe across a broad band of speeds" rather than a sharp single optimum.
- **Man AHL** (a real quant CTA, not academic) publishes an investor-education piece, "AHL Explains — Momentum" (https://www.man.com/insights/ahl-explains-momentum), which is Official-source for the practitioner claim that Man AHL runs "a suite of EWMA models at multiple speeds" simultaneously and combines them, rather than picking one "best" speed — their stated rationale (per search-summarized secondary characterization, not a direct primary read) is that faster EWMA speeds pick up trend emergence earlier (useful for crisis-alpha convexity) at the cost of higher turnover/transaction costs, while slower speeds are cheaper to trade but slower to react, and blending speeds is preferred to committing to one. This is a practitioner design choice, not a peer-reviewed robustness proof, but it is a real, named firm's disclosed methodology (Official-source that the firm says this; Inference on whether their live returns actually validate it, which they do not disclose).
- No standalone academic paper was found that is exclusively "AQR robustness study of EMA pair grid search" — the closest is Levine & Pedersen (#6) itself.
**Practical implication:** the honest state of the evidence is: one paper (#6, not fully verified in primary text) argues qualitative flatness across speeds, and one large CTA (Man AHL) discloses that its actual practice is to hold multiple speeds simultaneously rather than to pick a single optimized one. Both point the same direction — don't grid-search a single EMA pair to a backtest peak — but neither is a rigorous, replicated academic robustness study specific to single-name equity options. Flag this as the weakest-evidenced item in the list.

## Topic B — combining signals without overfitting

### 8. Timmermann (2006) and Clemen (1989)
**(a) Timmermann, A. (2006), "Forecast Combinations."** Handbook of Economic Forecasting, Vol. 1, Chapter 4, pp. 135–196 (eds. G. Elliott, C. Granger, A. Timmermann), Elsevier. DOI: 10.1016/S1574-0706(05)01004-9.
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1574070605010049 (working paper version: CEPR DP 5361)
**Finding:** Survey/synthesis chapter concluding that combining multiple forecasts empirically beats picking the single best model ex ante in most studies, and that this holds even for the simplest combination rule (equal-weighted average) that ignores correlation structure between the individual forecasts' errors — the gain from combining is highly robust, while gains from cleverer *optimal*-weight combination schemes are much less reliable out-of-sample.
**(b) Clemen, R.T. (1989), "Combining Forecasts: A Review and Annotated Bibliography."** International Journal of Forecasting, Vol. 5, No. 4, pp. 559–583. DOI: 10.1016/0169-2070(89)90012-5.
**URL:** https://people.duke.edu/~clemen/bio/Published%20Papers/13.CombiningReview-Clemen-IJOF-89.pdf (open-access author PDF); publisher: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v5y1989i4p559-583.html
**Finding:** Annotated review of 209 papers on forecast combination; concludes the evidence is "virtually unanimous" that combining forecasts improves accuracy, and — the specific point most relevant here — that simple averages are frequently as good as or better than more "sophisticated," estimated-optimal-weight combinations, an empirical puzzle the paper explicitly flags as unresolved ("what is the explanation for the robustness of the simple average of forecasts?").
**Practical implication:** Both are Official-source, well-corroborated, and directly on point: for combining several technical/momentum indicators (or several EMA speeds) into one composite signal on 4 names with limited data, the literature's consistent lesson is to prefer a simple equal-weighted combination over an in-sample-optimized weighting scheme — optimized weights tend to overfit small samples and lose the advantage out-of-sample.

### 9. DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal (2009)
**Title:** "Optimal Versus Naive Diversification: How Inefficient Is the 1/N Portfolio Strategy?"
**Authors:** Victor DeMiguel, Lorenzo Garlappi, Raman Uppal
**Venue:** The Review of Financial Studies, Vol. 22, No. 5 (May 2009), pp. 1915–1953. DOI: 10.1093/rfs/hhm075.
**URL:** https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/22/5/1915/1592901 ; SSRN: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1376199
**Finding:** Evaluated 14 different mean-variance-style "optimal" portfolio construction models (with various estimation-error corrections) against the naive equal-weighted (1/N) portfolio, out-of-sample, across 7 empirical datasets. None of the 14 optimized models consistently beat plain 1/N on Sharpe ratio, certainty-equivalent return, or turnover — the theoretical gains from optimization were more than offset by estimation error in the inputs (expected returns, covariances) needed to run the optimizer.
**Practical implication:** Directly analogous to #8 for portfolio/position weights rather than forecasts: with only 4 names and a short, non-stationary sample, an estimated "optimal" weighting across names or signals is very likely to be estimation-error-dominated; equal weighting is a defensible, literature-backed default until there's a lot more data to estimate a genuinely better weighting scheme.

### 10. Bailey & López de Prado — Deflated Sharpe Ratio and Probability of Backtest Overfitting
**(a) Bailey, D.H. & López de Prado, M. (2014), "The Deflated Sharpe Ratio: Correcting for Selection Bias, Backtest Overfitting, and Non-Normality."** The Journal of Portfolio Management, Vol. 40, No. 5 (40th Anniversary Special Issue), pp. 94–107. DOI: 10.3905/jpm.2014.40.5.094.
**URL:** https://jpm.pm-research.com/content/40/5/94.abstract ; SSRN (open): https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2460551
**Finding:** Proposes the Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR), which computes the probability that an observed Sharpe ratio is genuinely positive after explicitly correcting for (i) the number of independent strategy variants/trials searched over (selection bias under multiple testing) and (ii) non-normality (skew/kurtosis) of the return distribution, both of which mechanically inflate a naively reported Sharpe ratio.
**(b) Bailey, D.H., Borwein, J.M., López de Prado, M. & Zhu, Q.J. (2017; SSRN-circulated 2014), "The Probability of Backtest Overfitting."** Journal of Computational Finance, Vol. 20, No. 4, pp. 39–69.
**URL:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2326253 (a companion "pseudo-mathematics" plain-language version appeared in Notices of the AMS 61(5), 2014, pp. 458–471)
**Finding:** Introduces "combinatorially symmetric cross-validation" (CSCV), a model-free, nonparametric method to estimate the Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO) — i.e., the probability that a strategy selected as the in-sample best performer among many candidates will underperform the median candidate out-of-sample. Demonstrated on worked examples that PBO rises quickly with the number of strategy variants tried.
**Practical implication:** Both are Official-source and this repo already leans on this exact framework (`uv run python -m options_researcher.robustness`, DSR/PBO-style discipline referenced in memory). Directly relevant to indicators-optimization: if the work involves trying several EMA pairs, several combination weightings, or several thresholds and picking the best-backtested one, DSR/PBO is the correct tool to quantify how much of that "best" result is just multiple-testing luck before it's trusted or registered.

### 11. Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016)
**Title:** "…and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns"
**Authors:** Campbell R. Harvey, Yan Liu, Heqing Zhu
**Venue:** The Review of Financial Studies, Vol. 29, No. 1 (Jan 2016), pp. 5–68 (Editor's Choice). DOI: 10.1093/rfs/hhv059. Originally NBER Working Paper 20592.
**URL:** https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/29/1/5/1843824 ; https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592
**Finding:** Surveys the (at the time) 300+ published factors claimed to predict the equity cross-section and argues that, given how many factors have already been data-mined from the same datasets, the conventional significance bar (t-statistic > 2.0) is far too lenient; they build a multiple-testing-adjusted framework and historical cutoff series, concluding a newly proposed factor should need a t-statistic > 3.0 to be taken seriously, and that most claimed findings in financial economics are consequently likely false. (Note for completeness: this conclusion is contested — e.g., Chen (2021, working paper) and Chen & Zimmermann (2018) re-estimate false-discovery rates on the same/similar predictor sets and argue most published findings are actually likely true once publication bias is modeled differently; a low t-stat hurdle around 1.8 is defended in that later work. Both sides of this debate are Official-source-documented; the underlying t>3 hurdle recommendation and its rationale, from Harvey/Liu/Zhu directly, is the most load-bearing citation for this program's own use of a t>3-style bar.)
**Practical implication:** If any indicator or combination rule discovered here is later pitched as a "new factor" worth its own registration, the t>3 (not t>2) hurdle — and the broader message that any single positive backtest drawn from a search over many rule variants needs its significance bar raised in proportion to how many variants were tried — is the right discipline to apply before treating it as more than a candidate for further, pre-registered testing.

### 12. Moreira & Muir (2017) and the volatility-scaling critique literature
**Title:** "Volatility-Managed Portfolios"
**Authors:** Alan Moreira, Tyler Muir
**Venue:** The Journal of Finance, Vol. 72, No. 4 (2017), pp. 1611–1644. DOI: 10.1111/jofi.12513. Originally NBER Working Paper 22208 (2016).
**URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jofi.12513 ; https://www.nber.org/papers/w22208 ; open PDF: https://amoreira2.github.io/alan-moreira.github.io/VolPortfolios_published.pdf
**Finding:** Scaling a portfolio's exposure inversely to trailing (prior-month) realized variance — take less risk when recent volatility is high, more when it's low — produced large alphas and materially higher Sharpe ratios in-sample across the market factor and several other well-known factors (value, momentum, profitability, ROE, investment, betting-against-beta, currency carry). Reported utility gain for a mean-variance investor trading only the volatility-managed market portfolio: as much as +65% in certainty-equivalent lifetime utility versus the unmanaged portfolio, because realized-volatility changes are not offset by proportional changes in expected returns (i.e., Sharpe ratio itself rises with vol scaling, not just the return).
**Caveat/critique literature (Official-source):** Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang & Yan (2020), "On the Performance of Volatility-Managed Portfolios," Journal of Financial Economics 138(1), pp. 95–117, DOI: 10.1016/j.jfineco.2020.04.015 (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X2030132X ; SSRN: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3357038), tested 103 equity strategies and found volatility-managed versions do **not** systematically outperform unmanaged versions in direct, implementable out-of-sample comparisons; the positive "spanning regression" alphas Moreira & Muir report require an ex-post-optimal combination of the scaled and unscaled portfolio that isn't tradeable in real time, and the underlying regressions are structurally unstable. A subsequent Wiley/JoF paper (DeMiguel et al. 2024, "A Multifactor Perspective on Volatility-Managed Portfolios") and other work (cited as "Barroso and Detzel") reportedly find the effect further weakens once realistic transaction costs are applied — flagged here as Inference (found via secondary summary, not independently confirmed against the primary Barroso/Detzel text).
**Practical implication:** Directly relevant if position sizing is ever scaled by trailing realized/implied vol: the in-sample case for vol-scaling boosting Sharpe is real and well published, but the critique literature is equally real and specifically shows the effect is largely a spanning-regression/ex-post artifact that degrades or disappears once implementability and costs are enforced — exactly the kind of thing this repo's backtest-realism-audit discipline should stress-test before trusting a vol-scaled position-sizing rule.

## Topic C — what real quant firms publicly disclose about combining signals

**Honest framing up front:** none of the four firms below publish their actual production alpha-combination methodology. What's public is a mix of investor-marketing research (AQR, Man AHL — both run public/semi-public factor and CTA products, so they have a commercial incentive to publish credible-looking research), pure infrastructure/culture writing (Jane Street, D.E. Shaw), and third-party reporting/regulatory filings (none of which reveal methodology). Treat everything below as Official-source only for "this firm published/said this specific document," not for "this is how they actually trade."

- **Jane Street** — primarily an electronic market-maker and ETF authorized-participant/arbitrageur, not a published-alpha research shop. It has occasionally published *infrastructure* material (e.g., a 2017 talk on its internal "JX" exchange design) but does not publish trading-signal or alpha-combination methodology; public information about Jane Street's actual trading is limited to scale/revenue disclosures (e.g., press reporting of ~$7B 2024 revenue, market-share stats in Treasury ETFs and options) and, more recently, regulatory/litigation disclosures (SEBI's 2025 India action; a DOJ suit alleging Bitcoin-ETF-arbitrage-related manipulation) — none of which describe methodology, and the regulatory matters are allegations, not established findings. **VERIFICATION STATUS:** general characterization Official-source-consistent across multiple independent outlets; no methodology paper exists to cite because none is published.
- **AQR** — the most research-transparent of the four; publishes peer-reviewed and practitioner research under its own name, including #4–#7 and #12/critique threads above, all authored or co-authored by AQR principals (Pedersen, Ooi, Hurst, Levine, Moreira is not AQR but the vol-managed-portfolios literature overlaps AQR's factor-investing output elsewhere). This is real, citable, peer-reviewed-or-SSRN research — but it describes *general, license-agnostic* trend/momentum/combination principles (e.g., "trend signature" equivalence, diversify across speeds, equal-weight forecast combinations), not AQR's specific live parameter choices or capital allocation rules.
- **Man AHL** — second-most transparent; publishes investor-education pieces (e.g., "AHL Explains — Momentum," https://www.man.com/insights/ahl-explains-momentum) and occasional research papers under named authors (e.g., Hamill, Rattray & van Hemert (2016), "Trend Following: Equity and Bond Crisis Alpha"). These disclose real structural facts — e.g., that AHL runs a suite of EWMA models at multiple speeds combined together rather than one single optimized speed, and that they've publicly discussed reweighting away from crowded very-short-horizon signals over time — but not exact weights, thresholds, or risk-scaling constants.
- **D.E. Shaw** — the most opaque of the four regarding methodology; its public "Library" (deshaw.com/library) contains occasional pieces on data infrastructure (e.g., versioned datasets, avoiding look-ahead bias in point-in-time data) and market-structure commentary, explicitly not signal-combination or alpha methodology. Reporting consistently characterizes the firm as intentionally secretive about how its strategies actually work.

**Practical implication for this program:** the only *directly reusable* public research among these four is AQR's (already covered in Topic A/B above) and, to a lesser extent, Man AHL's disclosed structural principle of blending multiple signal speeds rather than picking one. Jane Street and D.E. Shaw contribute essentially nothing methodological to a single-name options trend/momentum question — citing them for alpha-combination technique would not be honest, and nothing here should be represented as if it reveals how any of the four firms actually trades.
